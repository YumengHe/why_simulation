2.10 Generation: multiple outputs with TRELLIS

C. Reconstruction and generation · TRELLIS

A pretrained 3D generator offers another route to a 3D asset, the object’s digital model. Give the system a cabinet image, and it uses knowledge learned from many objects to propose the cabinet’s shape and appearance in 3D.

This section introduces TRELLIS to show two ideas: prior training can help create an asset from limited input, and one generation system can support several representations. Its mesh, Gaussian, and radiance-field outputs use the representation and rendering concepts from Parts A and B.

The routes in 2.8 and 2.9 fit a cabinet’s representation to photographs of that scene. Here, we follow the generation route from an input image through TRELLIS to a supported 3D output.

2.10.1 From one image to a proposed 3D asset

Suppose only one photograph shows the cabinet’s front. It provides little direct evidence about the back or interior. A generator can use patterns learned during training to propose those unseen regions. The result is a plausible asset; hidden details still need inspection if we want to reproduce a particular real cabinet.

TRELLIS is an example of a 3D asset generator. Give its image-based model a cabinet picture, and it can create a 3D result using its prior training. It can output that result as a mesh, 3D Gaussians, or a radiance field. TRELLIS project.

The method and its output are different things: TRELLIS is the system that creates the asset; mesh, Gaussians, and radiance field describe ways to represent its result. These are the representations introduced in Section 2.1.

Mesh output

Vertices and connected faces describe surfaces. Appearance can be supplied with textures and materials.

Gaussian output

Positions, shapes, opacity, and appearance describe blobs that a Gaussian renderer blends into views.

Radiance field output

Color and density describe the scene at locations in 3D. To make a picture, provide the field and a virtual camera to a compatible volume renderer. It reads color and density along camera rays and combines them into pixels, as in Section 2.6.3.

For the cabinet, choose a camera on its right to render a right-side view. Keep the field unchanged; choose another camera to make another view.

The next tool depends on the output: a mesh editor or mesh renderer for meshes, a Gaussian viewer or renderer for Gaussians, and a compatible volume renderer for radiance fields. TRELLIS provides a renderer for its own radiance-field output. Its official example uses it to make a video from a sequence of camera views. The field’s saved form can differ from the original NeRF network described in Section 2.9.

2.10.2 Compare the representations

A generator can save a 3D result in different forms. The cabinet below illustrates what those forms look like: a mesh stores connected surfaces, while Gaussians store colored blobs in 3D.

These are scripted teaching views of a constructed cabinet, not TRELLIS predictions. See the TRELLIS gallery for the authors’ model results.

Both representations can show color. The mesh and Gaussian views here share a camera and color scheme. The triangle lines help us inspect the mesh; a renderer can hide those lines and use textures and materials to show its appearance.

Why would we choose a mesh?

Suppose we want a robot to grasp the cabinet handle. We need to locate the handle’s surface and the gap behind it, edit the shape if necessary, and prepare shapes for contact. A mesh gives us explicit surface patches to work with. We can also organize its geometry into parts before adding joints and physical properties.

If our immediate goal is to display the asset from new viewpoints, a Gaussian representation supplies colored blobs that can be projected and blended directly. The same rendering operation works for Gaussians produced by TRELLIS or fitted with 3DGS. The choice depends on what we want to do with the result; surface geometry and Gaussian appearance can also be used together. Original 3DGS project.
