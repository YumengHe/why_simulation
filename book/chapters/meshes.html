2.2 Meshes: representation

A. Representation

Mesh: Representation · 2.2 · Rendering · 2.5 · Reconstruction · 2.8.7

A mesh describes surface shape using connected patches. For a drawer, these can describe the outside, the inner walls, and the bottom. The empty space inside the drawer stays empty: a surface mesh does not mean the whole object is filled with material.

A point cloud records sampled positions in 3D. A mesh also records how positions connect to form surfaces. The positions are called vertices, their connections are edges, and the surface patches are faces. Moving the positions changes the shape; changing their connections changes how the surface is assembled.

Triangle and quad meshes on the same cabinet

Both are surface meshes. In a quad mesh, each face has four sides. In a triangle mesh, each face has three. The cabinet below uses the same corner positions and camera in both views; only the face connections change.

Why use triangles or quads?

Quads are useful for editing: rows of four-sided faces make it easier to select a strip of the cabinet surface and reshape it. Triangles are useful for rendering: three corners define a flat patch that software can project and draw. A common workflow is to edit with quads and let the software divide them into triangles for rendering. Blender’s triangulation tool.{{cite:blender_manual}}

Face type and amount of detail are two separate choices. A mesh can have a few large triangles or many small triangles; the same applies to quads. More, well-placed corners can follow a curved handle more closely, while more faces generally require more storage and processing. In the two cabinets above, splitting each flat quad into two triangles leaves the surface in exactly the same place.

Measured points can be turned into a mesh by estimating a connected surface. Section 2.8.7 explains where this surface-reconstruction step fits.

Why can a mesh look angular?

A round bar has a curved surface. A mesh builds that surface from flat pieces. Imagine joining flat strips around the bar: a few wide strips make obvious corners; many narrow strips follow the curve more closely.

Both models approximate the same round bar and use the same camera view. The lines show where the mesh faces meet.

This is why the number of faces can matter: more, smaller faces can represent a curve more closely, when their corners are placed along that curve. A flat cabinet panel needs much less detail because its surface is already flat.

More faces also mean more data to store and process. We choose enough to describe the shape we need; the highest face count is not automatically the best choice.

The same idea on a cabinet

These two cabinets have the same flat panels. Only the round handles change: each bar has 6 sides on the left and 32 sides on the right. The enlarged drawer handle makes the difference easier to see.

The camera and colors are the same in both pictures. The lines mark the edges of the triangular faces. The extra faces improve the curved handles; the flat cabinet panels stay unchanged.
