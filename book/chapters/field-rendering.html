2.6 Radiance fields: rendering with NeRF

B. Rendering

To draw the cabinet, choose a camera and query color and density along its lines of sight. Follow one ray through P1, P2, and P3, then repeat the calculation to form the whole image.

2.6.1 Rendering a radiance field

For a radiance field, begin with one image pixel. The camera defines a line of sight through that pixel into the scene. The renderer samples locations along that line and asks the field for color and density there.

Along a line toward the drawer, empty air contributes little. Locations near the blue front panel contribute blue and reduce the contribution from behind. Combining the answers produces the pixel’s color. Repeating this across the image produces a view of the cabinet.

Here we use the NeRF example introduced in Section 2.3.2. The network supplies color and density; the renderer supplies the sample locations and combines the answers.

The network’s saved numerical settings are called weights. They determine its answers. During this rendering step, we keep those settings fixed and query the network at different positions.

2.6.2 Generate the 3D positions from a camera pixel

Start with the camera’s position, orientation, and lens parameters. A virtual camera can supply these settings directly. When matching a photograph, COLMAP can estimate them, as described in Section 2.7.3. In either case, the renderer uses the camera settings to calculate sample positions and viewing directions:

  1. It selects a pixel in the image to be rendered.
  2. It uses the camera position, orientation, and lens parameters to calculate the pixel’s ray: a line of sight extending into the scene.
  3. It samples several distances along that ray. Each distance gives a 3D sample position.
  4. It sends each position and the ray’s direction to the network. Points on the same ray share the same direction.

A small coordinate example

Here, X points right, Y points toward the cabinet, and Z points up. Coordinates are in meters. The camera is at (0, −3, 0.64). The selected ray points straight along +Y, so its direction is (0, 1, 0).

For P2, start at the camera and move 2 m along +Y: the Y coordinate changes from −3 to −1. X and Z stay the same. Other pixels generally produce rays pointing in different directions. In practice, the renderer uses many samples, not just these three.

Each sampled 3D position, together with the ray’s viewing direction, forms an input to the NeRF neural network. For P2, the input is position (0, −1, 0.64) and direction (0, 1, 0). The network predicts a color and density for each sample. Section 2.6.3 explains how the renderer combines these predictions along the ray to produce one pixel’s color.

2.6.3 From network outputs to the selected pixel

Section 2.6.2 calculated P1, P2, and P3 along the ray for one selected image pixel. Each position and the shared viewing direction are supplied as input to the same NeRF network. The network uses its current weights to calculate a color and density for each sample as output.

A prediction that represents this cabinet well assigns low density to the air and blue color with high density near the drawer front:

P1 · air
Low density gives this sample little influence on the pixel’s color.
P2 · air
Low density again: most of the view continues toward the drawer.
P3 · drawer
Blue color and high density: samples around the front panel supply most of the pixel’s color and reduce the contribution from farther behind.

The renderer combines the sample colors from near to far. Density and the distance represented by each sample determine how strongly it contributes and how much of the view passes through to later samples. This calculation is called volume rendering. It uses many samples along the ray; P1–P3 are three labeled locations within that process. Rendering details in the paper.

The combined color is written into the original selected pixel. In this example, the drawer front makes that pixel blue.

From one pixel to a complete cabinet image

The renderer repeats the same calculation for every pixel, using a different ray for each one. The result is an ordinary 2D color image, like this cabinet view:

One ray gives one pixel. The ray through P1–P3 gives the blue pixel discussed above.

All the rays give the whole picture. Rays through the drawer produce blue pixels; rays through the door produce gold pixels; rays through the body produce teal pixels. Rays that pass through empty space leave the white background visible.

Color and density work together. Color supplies the possible colors along a ray. Density helps determine how much each sample contributes and how much is hidden behind it. After combining them, the finished pixel contains a single color.

This completes one render: the field and camera have produced a 2D image. During reconstruction, the same calculation becomes part of a training loop. Section 2.9.3 compares the rendered pixels with a photograph and updates the network.
