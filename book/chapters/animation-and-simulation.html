4.1.3 Back to the drawer

The cabinet GIFs in Chapter 3 work like the box on the left. We choose the drawer’s opening distance, q, at each moment: closed, a little open, farther open, and back again.

To simulate a pull, we give the drawer mass, describe friction, and apply a pulling force. Its joint still restricts it to sliding. The simulator calculates how fast it moves and how its opening distance changes. The examples below do this.

4.1.4 Pull the drawer

A force is a push or a pull. We measure it in newtons, written N. A 3 N pull is three times as strong as a 1 N pull. It does not tell us how many centimeters the drawer will open.

Here we pull each drawer for half a second, then let go. Both start closed and still. Both have a mass of 2 kg, and we leave out friction for this first comparison.

We chose the force, not the ending position. The opening distances come from calculating the motion and drawing each resulting position.

These are side-view teaching examples with chosen values, not measurements of the earlier cabinet. The cabinet stays fixed, and its rails keep the drawer moving horizontally. Only forces along the slide are shown. The GIFs play slowly and hold their last frame before replaying.

4.1.5 Same pull, different mass

Now keep the pull the same: 3 N for half a second. Change only the moving mass, from 2 kg to 4 kg. Think of adding items to the drawer. Friction is still left out so we can see the effect of mass by itself.

This is why the simulator needs mass. Two drawers can look identical and have the same sliding joint, yet respond differently to the same pull.

4.1.6 Friction can resist the pull

Have you pulled a drawer gently and found that it would not budge? Friction can balance a weak pull and keep the drawer still. Once the drawer slides, friction acts against that sliding movement.

Both drawers below have a mass of 2 kg and receive the same 3 N pull. Only the friction in their rails changes.

The easy-sliding drawer starts moving, with 0.8 N of friction opposing the 3 N pull. The stiff rails can resist a pull of up to 4 N before sliding starts. With our 3 N pull, their friction only needs to provide 3 N in the opposite direction. The forces balance, so that drawer stays still.

Applying a force does not guarantee movement. We have to account for the other forces too.

4.1.7 What happens after letting go?

Removing the pull does not instantly stop a moving drawer. In this example, both drawers are already moving outward at 40 cm per second. They are both 10 cm open when we let go.

The drawer does not automatically close when we let go. That would need something else, such as a spring or a closing force. Here, each replay simply restarts the example.

The sliding joint still has one degree of freedom: q tells us the opening distance. The force changes how q develops over time; it does not add another degree of freedom.
