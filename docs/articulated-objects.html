    3.1 What is an articulated object?

    An articulated object is made of parts that can move relative to one another. A drawer slides. A door swings. The cabinet holds them together.

    The cabinet below has two independent movements: the drawer slides and the door rotates. Its connections determine which movements are possible.

    3.1.1 Links and joints

    We describe this cabinet using two building blocks: links and joints.

    Link
    A rigid part: its shape stays the same as it moves. The body, drawer, and door are three links.
    Joint
    A connection that defines how two links can move relative to each other. The door’s hinge connects the door to the body and lets it rotate.

    Both handles are fixed: the drawer handle belongs to the drawer link, and the door handle belongs to the door link. Each moves together with its part.

    3.1.2 How joints let parts move

    The drawer and door move in different ways. Move the sliders to try each joint.

    The allowed range is the joint’s motion limit. The teaching cabinet allows the drawer to open from 0 to 76 cm and the door from 0° to 90°.

    3.1.3 Degrees of freedom

    One articulated object can have several degrees of freedom. This cabinet is one object, but it has two movable joints: one for the drawer and one for the door.

    A degree of freedom (DOF) is an independent motion you can control. With the cabinet body held fixed:

    PartIndependent controlDOF
    DrawerHow far it slides1
    DoorHow far it rotates1
    Whole cabinetDrawer and door move independently2

    You can leave the door still and move the drawer. You can also leave the drawer still and move the door. So describing the whole cabinet’s pose takes two numbers: the drawer’s opening distance and the door’s opening angle.

    Here, each joint has 1 DOF; the whole cabinet has 2 DOF. Opening the drawer farther changes its position, but it still has the same one degree of freedom.

    3.1.4 Kinematic trees

    A kinematic tree records which parts are connected. Each node is a link; each connecting line is a joint.

    The body is the root: the starting link of this tree. It is the parent of the drawer and door; they are its children. The tree describes connections, not the parts’ positions on the page.

    The same cabinet has two movable joints: a slide for the drawer and a hinge for the door. Each handle is fixed to its part and moves with it. Select a part in the tree, then play its motion.

    Drawer. Slides relative to the body. Its handle stays attached and moves with it.

    Parts on separate branches move independently

    The drawer and door are on separate branches of the body. Moving the drawer leaves the door in place; opening the door leaves the drawer in place. Both handles belong to their respective links, so they add no separate movement. The cabinet still has 2 DOF.

    Not every articulated object forms a tree. Some mechanisms contain closed loops. This example focuses on a tree, where each link other than the root has one parent.
